Harmonic Functions

Mentor: Mustafa Omer (Year 4)

About the mentor

I'm a 4th year mathematics undergrad. I am interested in complex analysis and differential geometry.

Description

We will study harmonic functions, which are the solutions to Laplace's equation. We will investigate the connections between harmonic and holomorphic function. In particular, one of the most surprising facts about harmonic functions is that they are locally the real part of holomorphic functions. We will explore some fundamental results, including the mean value property, the maximum principle, and Harnack's principle. Towards the end, we will discuss the Dirichlet Problem.

The goal is to develop an understanding of these concepts through proofs and examples, exploring the connections between complex analysis and partial differential equations. We will roughly follow Axler, Bourdon, and Ramey's Harmonic Function Theory.

Prerequisites: A course in multivariable calculus. Having some complex analysis background would be helpful but not necessary.

Combinatorial game theory

Mentor: Hana Connelly (Year 4)

About the mentor

My name is Hana, I'm in my fourth and final year specializing in Mathematics and its Applications (stats stream). I really enjoy proof-based math and creative problem solving! Through my experience in MAT475 (Problem Solving Seminar), I have developed a strong interest in exploring how mathematical ideas can be used to solve unfamiliar problems. I would love to share this enthusiasm with other students through a reading project in combinatorial game theory while continuing to deepen my own understanding of the subject. My goal is to pursue further studies in math and hopefully get a PhD in pure mathematics!

Description

This project explores the mathematics of two-player games in which players take turns making moves according to specific rules. Through games such as Nim and other impartial games, students will investigate winning and losing positions, develop strategic arguments, and learn to prove when a winning strategy exists. Topics will include mathematical induction, invariants, recursive reasoning, and the Sprague–Grundy theorem, which provides a framework for analyzing and combining impartial games. Emphasis will be placed on creative problem solving, rigorous proof construction, and discovering the mathematical structures underlying strategic games. Students will conclude by independently analyzing a game or exploring a generalization of a known result.

Prerequisites: MAT137Y1/MAT157Y1; MAT223H1 or equivalent; MAT344 would be very useful but not required.

Look at the sky : The n-th body problem

Mentor: Slimane Alaoui Soulimani Valenti (Year 3)

About the mentor

I'm Slimane Alaoui Soulimani Valenti, a third year Math major at UofT.

Description

Look at the night sky and you'll see planets tracing paths astronomers can predict centuries ahead. But the solar system has eight planets and a sun, at least nine bodies all pulling on each other. Meanwhile, one of the most famous results in mathematics says even the three-body problem has no general closed-form solution, and its motion can be chaotic. So how do we predict anything at all?

We begin with the two-body problem, which reduces completely using conserved quantities and gives Kepler's conics. For n ≥ 3, we ask what goes wrong. Using the Hamiltonian formulation and Noether's theorem, we identify the classical first integrals (energy, momentum, angular momentum) and use them to reduce the system's dimension. We then see why this isn't enough: Bruns and Poincaré showed there are no further integrals of the right kind, and Poincaré's work on the restricted three-body problem led directly to the discovery of chaos.

Prerequisites: Currently taking a course with proofs. If possible, I would like either all of the "students" to either have the same experience with ODEs (not at all wouldn't be a problem as I could just add a session).

Hours per week: 2 · Would consider continuing in the winter: Yes

Rankin-Selberg theory

Mentor: Zander (Year 4)

About the mentor

Hi I'm Zander. I'm a third year math specialist. I am interested in all kinds of math, and especially like to get my hands on objects in order to understand them better.

Description

The prime number theorem is a central result in algebraic number theory. It approximates the asymptotic growth of the count of prime numbers. One classical approach to its proof uses Tauberian theorems to connect the behavior of Dirichlet series near their singularities to asymptotic information about their coefficients. This provides a natural starting point for exploring a broader principle: analytic properties of L-functions encode their arithmetic information.

The goal of this reading course will to first understand this proof of the prime number theorem. And then we will move to Rankin-Selberg theory. By studying L-functions associated with automorphic forms, we will investigate how ideas from the classical theory extend to more general arithmetic settings.

Prerequisites: MAT137/157, MAT224/247. Please be enrolled in MAT257 or MAT237 and M327.

Hours per week: 2 · Would consider continuing in the winter: Yes

Free Random Variables

Mentor: Zef (Year 4)

About the mentor

4th year, math specialist student interested in infinite dimensional linear algebra and analysis.

Description

Why do the eigenvalues of a large symmetric random matrix form a semicircle? We will cover the basics of operator algebras and quickly review central notions from classical probability. The focus of the project is free independence and free products. Our goal is to prove Wigner's semicircle law. We will follow Nica and Speicher, Lectures on the Combinatorics of Free Probability.

Prerequisites: MAT157 and MAT247. Some knowledge of probability is desirable.

Hours per week: 1 · Would consider continuing in the winter: Yes
